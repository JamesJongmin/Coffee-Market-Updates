Coffee Futures Market Weekly Update

This was the week the “27-year-low certified stocks” narrative — the last pillar of the bull case for more than a year — physically collapsed. ICE certified arabica inventories jumped from 217,646 bags on Tuesday to 247,887 bags on Thursday, a gain of roughly 30,000 bags (+13.9%) in three sessions. December arabica (KCZ26) fell to 276.50c on Thursday, taking the drawdown from the recent peak beyond -20% on an intraday basis, before rebounding +1.45% on Friday to close at 280.50c. The week finished at -5.20c (-1.82%). Paradoxically, the same week delivered hail across flowering coffee in southern Minas Gerais and the Cerrado — the first tangible upside risk in months — and the market barely priced it.

Week in Review

The September 5 report named “the point at which ICE certified stocks turn positive” as the single most important thing to watch, and cited Rabobank's view that “no meaningful rebuild is likely before November.” That turn happened this week — roughly six weeks ahead of the Rabobank timeline. Stocks slipped to 217,646 bags (-811) on Tuesday, still near a 27-year low, then rose +15,833 to 233,479 bags on Wednesday and +14,408 to 247,887 bags on Thursday — a gain of 30,241 bags (+13.9%) in two sessions. Wednesday alone saw 16,210 bags pass grading while 2,960 bags of Brazilian coffee were rejected, with close to 49,000 bags still pending certification.

Price front-ran the turn. December fell -6.85c (-2.36%) on Tuesday, -2.10c (-0.7%) on Wednesday to a 75-day low, and -1.79% on Thursday, trading as low as 274.60c intraday. CocoaIntel reported that the break below 275.11c pushed the drawdown from the recent peak beyond -20% on an intraday basis. On a settlement basis, December had lost 53.85c, or about 16%, over the 15 sessions from 335.50c on August 25 through Wednesday. Friday's +4.00c (+1.45%) bounce looks like an oversold correction: it recovered the 279.93c first resistance but failed to reach the 283–286c band.

What makes the week unusual is that a genuine bullish event actually occurred. On Tuesday, hail hit southern Minas Gerais and the Cerrado Mineiro — at least ten municipalities including Varginha, São Tomé das Letras, Alfenas, Três Corações, Campos Gerais, Araxá and Patrocínio. The timing lands squarely in the flowering window, which feeds directly into the May 2027 harvest. The Association of Rural Unions of Southern Minas Gerais estimates around 200 properties affected, losses of at least R$50 million and 15–20% damage in affected areas. The market nonetheless fell -1.79% on Thursday with that news on the tape. The collapse of the stock narrative simply overwhelmed the hail story — and that is the essence of the week.

The week's core dynamic: The last three weeks of decline were about a deteriorating supply outlook. This week's decline was about the physical disappearance of the bull case. Certified stocks had been, for more than a year, the only physical evidence that “the coffee isn't there.” That evidence grew 13.9% in three sessions. The September 5 report's framing that “certified stocks are a logistics indicator, not a procurement indicator” was confirmed this week by physical delivery — the coffee was never absent; it was in transit from Brazil, and it has now arrived.

News Briefing

  • (Mon 9/14, Secex) Brazilian green coffee exports reached roughly 107,900 tonnes (about 1.8 million bags) in the first eight working days of September, a daily pace of 13,500 tonnes, up 51.5% year on year. Revenue reached US$584.6 million (about US$73.1 million per working day), confirming that post-harvest volume is genuinely moving into international channels.
  • (Mon 9/14, Expocacer) The Cerrado Mineiro arabica harvest reached 99% by September 11, with about 90% already processed. Average conversion was 455 litres per 60-kg bag, among the better results of recent years. However, roughly 30% of harvested volume was collected from the ground, and the average sorting rate was about 21%, which weighs on the conversion into commercial grades.
  • (Mon 9/14, Expocacer) Roughly 33% of the region's total flowering potential has already bloomed early in the Cerrado Mineiro. Flowering has been stronger and more uniform on fields that carried little production this season, with the remainder expected across October and November. Multiple flowering events imply uneven ripening and more harvest passes, raising quality variability.
  • (Tue 9/15, Brazil weather) Hail damage across southern Minas and the Cerrado. INMET issued a yellow storm alert covering all of Espírito Santo plus parts of Minas Gerais, Rio de Janeiro and southern Bahia. One producer in Boa Esperança with an 80-hectare farm estimated the storm could cut his May 2027 harvest by around 30%.
  • (Wed 9/16, ICE) Certified arabica stocks rose +15,833 bags to 233,479, with 16,210 bags passing grading, 2,960 bags of Brazilian coffee rejected (about 15% of the 19,170 bags presented) and close to 49,000 bags pending. Thursday added a further +14,408 bags to 247,887.
  • (Wed 9/16, Cecafé) Brazilian shipments reached roughly 1.45 million bags through September 16, up about 10% on the comparable pace — arabica 1.06 million, robusta 313,000 and soluble about 76,000 bags. Certificate-of-origin requests exceeded 2.07 million bags, up more than 47%, pointing to a heavy second-half shipment pipeline.
  • (Thu 9/17, Cooxupé) Harvesting across the co-operative's area was 97.3% complete as of September 11, with São Paulo at 98.9% and Matas de Minas at 90%. Cooxupé spans more than 370 municipalities and 22,000 farming families, making it a leading indicator of supply availability.
  • (Thu 9/17, Sucafina via Reuters) Colombia's 2026/27 main crop is expected to be broadly similar in size to the previous season but with slightly lower quality, some insect pressure and continued earthquake recovery. USDA projects the new crop at 13.4 million bags versus 12.5 million previously. Larger volumes arrive around November and December.
  • (9/16–17, Abics) Brazilian soluble coffee exports reached 7,700 tonnes (about 333,700 bags) in August, up 24.9% year on year. January–August shipments totalled 65,300 tonnes (2.83 million bags), up 12.8%, while domestic consumption rose to 19,700 tonnes (+11.9%).
  • (9/16–17, Brazilian physicals) Indicative prices for good-quality coffee in southern Minas Gerais eased from R$1,750 per 60-kg bag on Tuesday to R$1,730 on Thursday, with reports that sellers remain reluctant to transact after the futures decline.

Fundamental Analysis

1. Stocks — the narrative did not break; the coffee actually arrived

This is the single decisive event of the week. Certified stocks, at roughly 218,000 bags on September 11 and near multi-decade lows, rebounded from 217,646 on Tuesday to 233,479 on Wednesday and 247,887 on Thursday. The cause is not ambiguous: large trading houses moved Brazilian arabica to exchange warehouses for grading, and the coffee started to pass. Wednesday alone accounted for 16,210 bags, with about 49,000 bags still queued.

The September 5 report, citing Rabobank, argued that “low certified stocks are an indicator of delayed arrival, not of absent supply.” This week was the decisive test of that proposition. The coffee existed, Brazilian logistics bottlenecks eased, and it entered the warehouses. As of this week, the “27-year-low stocks” headline has exhausted its usefulness as a bullish argument.

Implication: The rebound is not merely a number; it is a recovery in exchange-deliverable supply. That (1) structurally pressures the nearby inversion, (2) materially reduces short-squeeze risk and (3) lowers the cost of carrying a speculative short. The curve confirms it: the December/March spread traded as low as 7.35c during the week and stood at 8.20c on Wednesday versus 8.80c a day earlier, while December/May narrowed from 13.60c to 11.35c. The curve remains inverted — evidence of residual tightness — but the direction is unmistakably flatter. For buyers, the value of paying a nearby premium to secure immediate delivery fell sharply this week.

2. Weather — the first real upside risk in months

The September 5 report concluded that “the September–October flowering risk premium has been erased, and the next upside window is Q1 2027.” That window opened far earlier, and in a different form. On Tuesday, September 15, hail fell across southern Minas and the Cerrado, with accumulation in parts of the Araxá region heavy enough to cover roads.

The problem is timing. Per Expocacer, the Cerrado Mineiro had already seen about 33% of its total flowering potential bloom early. Damage to flowers at this stage directly reduces the cherries that can develop for the May 2027 harvest. Hail also wounds branches and leaves, raising disease vulnerability and remediation cost. The Association of Rural Unions of Southern Minas Gerais estimates around 200 properties, at least R$50 million in losses and 15–20% damage in affected areas; one 80-hectare grower expects -30% for 2027.

On the other side sits overwhelming rainfall. Climatempo reported Minas Gerais weekly rain at 59.4 mm, or 1,212% of the historical average — a step beyond the Somar reading of 8.9 mm (127% of normal) cited in the September 5 report. Cumulative September rain in Patrocínio reached 43.1 mm by September 11 versus 35.9 mm a year earlier. Rain is unambiguously supportive of flower set.

Implication: Brazilian weather this week was a combination of localised damage (hail) and broad support (rain). The market priced only the latter. Two things deserve weight. First, the 2027 crop falls in the weak phase of the biennial cycle — its capacity to absorb losses is inherently small. Second, as Expocacer noted, multiple flowering events (33% now, the rest in October–November) produce uneven ripening and raise quality variability. Layer on Cemaden's warning of up to six heat-wave episodes before year-end under El Niño conditions, and the “heavy rain then extreme heat” sequence is considerably more dangerous than either event alone. The practical conclusion is that 2027 upside optionality got cheaper this week — the risk rose while the price fell.

This week's export data invite conflicting readings. Secex customs figures put green coffee exports in the first eight working days of September at +51.5% year on year on a daily-pace basis. Cecafé shipment data, by contrast, traced 169,144 bags through September 9 (-52.2%), 1,009,416 bags through September 14 (-12.7%) and roughly 1.45 million bags through September 16 (up about 10%) — a sharply improving trajectory within the month.

The gap is a timing artefact rather than a contradiction. Secex captures customs registration while Cecafé captures physical shipment, so mid-month discrepancies widen with documentation, cargo registration and vessel departure lags. The more meaningful signal is that certificate-of-origin requests exceeded 2.07 million bags, up more than 47%. Even at the September 14 checkpoint, requests (1.64 million bags) exceeded reported shipments (1.01 million) by more than 630,000 bags. There is substantially more coffee waiting to ship than has shipped.

Implication: That pipeline is the physical source of the certified-stock rebound. Late September and October set up for shipments and grading arriving simultaneously, with 49,000 bags already queued. In other words, the stock build is more likely a multi-week flow than a one-off event. The caveat is Expocacer's 30% ground-collected volume and 21% sorting rate, a reminder that harvested volume and grade-passing volume are different quantities — Wednesday's 2,960 bags of Brazilian rejections is the evidence.

4. Robusta — $3,400 gone, rejected at the 200-day

London robusta November fell from $3,525 the prior Friday to $3,481 (-54) on Tuesday, $3,430 (-51) on Wednesday and $3,391 (-39, -1.14%) on Thursday — three consecutive declines. Through Thursday that is -$134 (-3.80%), a larger fall than arabica's -1.82%. Technically, the contract was rejected at the 200-day moving average near $3,540, then lost the $3,500 psychological level and $3,434 support in turn.

The bearish driver is improving Brazilian conilon and robusta availability, which is eroding the premium embedded in London prices. Cecafé's September data confirm 313,000 bags of robusta shipped.

Vietnam, by contrast, remains tight. Domestic prices were around VND 95,000–95,500/kg on September 12, reflecting limited old-crop availability ahead of the new harvest. Forecasts then called for moderate to heavy rain across the Central and Southern Highlands from September 18 to 21, with warnings for lightning, hail, strong wind and isolated tornadoes between September 16 and 26. Excessive rain close to harvest can drive cherry drop, restricted field access and slower drying.

Stock divergence update: The September 5 report tracked the extreme contrast of arabica at a 27-year low versus robusta at a 9-month high for a third week and concluded that spread bets built on that divergence had failed empirically. This week the divergence itself began to resolve, because arabica stocks rebounded 13.9%. The New York–London differential narrowed from 125.85c to 124.85c accordingly. Every relative-value position premised on the stock gap lost its premise this week.

5. Positioning and technicals — deeply oversold, and still no buyers

The defining feature of this week's price action is “deeply oversold with no bid.” December lost 53.85c (about 16%) over the 15 sessions from 335.50c on August 25 to Wednesday, and passed a -20% drawdown from the recent peak intraday on Thursday. Over the past eight sessions the contract has travelled a range of nearly 24 cents with daily swings averaging close to 10 cents — the classic signature of a market that has not found a floor.

Yet every rally met selling. The contract traded down to 277.05c on Wednesday and 274.60c on Thursday before recovering into the close, repeatedly. Volume was about 24,000 lots Tuesday, 25,976 Wednesday and about 24,000 Thursday — the decline proceeded without an exceptional surge in participation, which looks more like systematic repricing than panic liquidation.

Implication: Technically, first support sits at 273.83c, then 271.17c and 267.73c. Resistance runs 279.93c (recovered Friday), 283.37c and 286.03c, and that cluster must be cleared before the decline can be described as stabilising. A sustained break below 275c opens the 264–257c zone. That said, the combination of deep oversold conditions, a new upside catalyst in the hail damage and a certified-stock rebuild that is already largely priced is precisely the fuel for a sharp corrective rally. The risk-reward on chasing shorts deteriorated markedly this week.

6. Physicals — futures are falling faster than origin

Despite the futures slide, Brazilian producers have not sold aggressively. Good-quality southern Minas coffee moved from R$1,750 per 60-kg bag on Tuesday to R$1,730 on Thursday — a decline of just 1.1%, against 2.5% for New York December over the same span. Thursday's physical market was described as quiet, with sellers showing limited willingness to transact after the decline.

The divergence means two things. First, differentials are widening — a falling terminal price does not translate one-for-one into lower procurement cost. Second, producers holding a record crop retain some pricing power. For a record crop to translate into physical pressure, growers must become financially compelled sellers, and that condition is not currently met.

7. Currency and macro — the real's cushion is gone

The September 5 report described USD/BRL at 5.10–5.13 as “the only bearish cushion.” That cushion was removed this week. The real retreated from roughly 5.08 on September 11 — a more than one-month high — to 5.1419 on September 18.

The driver is a narrowing rate differential. Brazil's central bank cut the Selic by 25 bp to 13.75% at its final meeting before the October presidential election, while the Federal Reserve raised its target range by 25 bp to 3.75–4.00%. August Brazilian inflation easing from 4.44% to 4.22% created room for the cut, but the net effect was carry compression from both sides. October election polling remains an additional source of volatility.

Implication: A weaker real raises the incentive for Brazilian producers to sell dollar-denominated coffee. It creates conditions under which growers sitting on record inventories can defend an R$1,730 local-currency floor while still increasing dollar-equivalent sales. In other words, the “producers are holding” support identified above can weaken through the currency channel. Through the October Brazilian election, the direction of USD/BRL within 5.10–5.20 is a primary variable for fourth-quarter origin selling pressure.

Implications

1. Retire every piece of research that uses certified stocks as a bull argument: Two weeks after the September 5 report characterised certified stocks as a logistics indicator rather than a procurement indicator, stocks rose 13.9% in three sessions. Rabobank's “no meaningful rebuild before November” was wrong by roughly six weeks. The metric to track is no longer the level of stocks but the weekly grading throughput and rejection rate — Wednesday's 19,170 bags presented produced 16,210 passed and 2,960 Brazilian bags rejected — a rejection rate near 15%, and that is the new number that matters.

2. This was the cheapest week yet for 2027 upside optionality: It is rare for real risk to rise while price falls, and that is what happened. Hail during flowering, a down-year in the biennial cycle, uneven ripening from multiple blooms and Cemaden's warning of up to six heat waves all point at 2027 production. The market ignored all of it. Low-cost calls with May–September 2027 expiries now carry a better asymmetry than at any point this quarter. The honest caveat: the hail damage remains localised and is not evidence of a broad regional crop failure.

3. The gap between futures hedging and physical procurement is widening: New York December fell 2.5% from Tuesday to Thursday while southern Minas physicals fell just 1.1% — differentials are widening. Combine Expocacer's 30% ground-collected volume and 21% sorting rate with the exchange's roughly 15% Brazilian rejection rate, and the gap between “coffee harvested” and “coffee that passes grade” becomes quantifiable. The September 5 judgement that a futures short hedges flat price but not the cost of higher-grade procurement was reconfirmed by this week's data.

4. A flattening curve changes the Q4 buying strategy: The December/March spread traded as low as 7.35c during the week and December/May narrowed from 13.60c to 11.35c. The curve is still inverted, meaning the nearby retains a premium, but the direction is easing. If the stock rebuild continues, the reason to pay that nearby premium disappears. Practically, the urgency of locking December delivery falls and the relative attraction of spreading into March and May rises.

5. The risk-reward on short positions has deteriorated: The September 5 report noted that 297.45c was already below the roughly 300c Reuters poll year-end target and concluded that “the direction is down but the entry point has worsened.” A further -5.7% has since printed. At 280.50c, the market sits nearly 7% below consensus. Add a -20% drawdown from the recent peak, deep oversold readings, a new bullish weather catalyst and reluctant origin selling. The trend remains lower, but a new short at this level accepts a thin margin against an asymmetric upside tail.

Outlook & Risks

Near-term structure (1–3 months)

December's configuration is “a downtrend attempting an oversold bounce.” Support runs 273.83c, 271.17c and 267.73c, with the 264–257c band below that. Resistance runs 279.93c (recovered Friday), 283.37c and 286.03c, and without a recovery of 290c a trend change is not arguable. The 300c level is now distant resistance.

Technical view: The close-to-close range this week was 14.00c (276.50–290.50c), similar to the last published week (16.15c), but intraday volatility was far higher — a roughly 24-cent range over eight sessions with daily swings averaging about 10 cents. The immediate question is whether Friday's +1.45% recovery of the 279.93c resistance extends. Two or more closes above the 283–286c cluster would turn this into a genuine oversold rally; a renewed break of 275c puts 267c in play. Thursday's 274.60c intraday low is the reference level for the coming weeks.

Medium-term outlook (3–12 months)

The medium-term bias remains lower, but the character of that bias has changed. The three bearish pillars set out on September 5 — StoneX's survey-confirmed 77.2 million-bag Brazilian crop, normalised flowering-season rainfall and Rabobank's 8.9 million-bag surplus — all remain intact, and this week added a fourth: the certified-stock rebuild. That rebuild removed the last bullish anchor left in the cycle.

Offsetting factors, however, have multiplied: (1) a down-year biennial cycle compounded by flowering hail damage (around 200 properties in southern Minas, estimated 15–20% damage in affected areas), (2) uneven ripening and quality variability from multiple flowering events, (3) Cemaden's warning of up to six heat waves before year-end, (4) Colombian quality deterioration and insect pressure (Sucafina), (5) tight Vietnamese old-crop stocks and pre-harvest heavy rain risk, and (6) differential support from reluctant producer selling. The base case is “continued downward pressure into Q4 as the stock rebuild and the Brazilian shipment rush land → confirmation of Colombian and Vietnamese new-crop arrivals in November–December → repricing on Brazilian summer weather in Q1 2027.”

Key risk factors

  • ICE certified stock rebuild (bearish, primary): 217,646 bags on 9/15 to 247,887 on 9/17 (+13.9%), with about 49,000 bags pending. The last physical pillar of the year-long bull case is gone.
  • Brazilian shipment pipeline (bearish): Secex +51.5% over the first eight working days of September; Cecafé certificate requests above 2.07 million bags (+47%). Volume keeps arriving into late September and October.
  • Flowering hail damage (bullish, new): At least ten municipalities across southern Minas and the Cerrado, around 200 properties, at least R$50 million, an estimated 15–20% loss in affected areas. Overlaid on a down-year biennial cycle with little capacity to absorb losses. Localised so far.
  • Heavy rain followed by heat (bullish/uncertain): Minas weekly rain of 59.4 mm (1,212% of normal) ahead of Cemaden's warning of up to six heat waves before year-end would be negative for fruit set and early cherry development.
  • Robusta (bearish): November at $3,391 (9/17), having lost $3,400 and $3,434 support after rejection at the $3,540 200-day average. Improving Brazilian conilon supply is eroding the London premium.
  • Vietnamese weather (bullish/limited): Moderate to heavy rain across the Central and Southern Highlands 9/18–21 and lightning, hail and wind warnings 9/16–26. With old-crop stocks thin (VND 95,000–95,500/kg), even modest disruption carries outsized nearby impact.
  • USD/BRL (turned bearish): Roughly 5.08 on 9/11 to 5.1419 on 9/18. A 25 bp Selic cut to 13.75% alongside a 25 bp Fed hike to 3.75–4.00% compressed carry. A weaker real stimulates origin selling.
  • Quality and grade risk (structural): Expocacer's 30% ground-collected volume and 21% sorting rate; the exchange's 2,960 Brazilian bags rejected out of 19,170 presented (about 15%). The gap between harvested and grade-passing volume is now quantified.
  • Oversold conditions and squeeze risk (technical): A -20% intraday drawdown from the recent peak and -53.85c (-16%) over 15 sessions on a settlement basis. The margin for new shorts has thinned.

What to watch next week

  • Daily ICE certified stock changes — the rate at which the 49,000-bag pending queue clears and the Brazilian rejection rate (about 15% this week). Holding above 250,000 bags is the first threshold.
  • Field assessments of the southern Minas and Cerrado hail — whether the 200-property estimate expands or contracts. Expansion would be the trigger for a 2027 balance-sheet repricing.
  • Whether 279.93–286.03c resistance is cleared — the dividing line between an oversold bounce and a continuing trend.
  • Cecafé final September shipments and certificate conversion — how much of the 2.07 million bags of requests actually ships, and whether the Secex–Cecafé gap converges at month-end.
  • The October second flowering wave — whether the remaining bloom Expocacer flagged develops uniformly. The spread of multiple blooms will determine 2027 quality variability.
  • Observed rainfall in Vietnam's Central and Southern Highlands — whether the 9/18–21 forecast actually disrupted harvest and drying. This ties directly to whether robusta reclaims $3,400.
  • CFTC COT (September 15 and 22) — the data this report could not confirm. The scale of new short accumulation below 280c determines squeeze vulnerability.
  • USD/BRL direction within 5.10–5.20 — the real's path alongside October election polling. Continued weakness increases origin selling pressure.

Conclusion

This was the week the bull case lost its last piece of physical evidence. Over the past year the justification for higher coffee prices changed repeatedly — Brazilian drought, frost, harvest delays, flowering failure. As each was disproved, one line survived: “ICE certified stocks are at a 27-year low.” It survived because it was an irrefutable physical fact. This week that fact changed. Stocks rose 30,241 bags (+13.9%) in three sessions, with 49,000 more in the queue.

The September 5 report argued that “low certified stocks indicate delayed arrival, not absent supply.” This week supplied the empirical proof. The coffee was not missing; it was sitting in Brazilian ports and logistics queues, and alongside the +51.5% daily shipment pace reported by Secex, it arrived. Rabobank's firm “no rebuild before November” was invalidated six weeks early.

And yet the same week produced an event pointing the other way. Hail fell on flowering coffee in southern Minas and the Cerrado, and the 2027 crop sits in the down-year of the biennial cycle. This is the first upside risk in months that actually happened rather than being hypothesised. The market nonetheless carried that news through a -20% drawdown from the recent peak on Thursday. That is the most important information the week produced — the market is currently trading only the coffee that exists now, and assigning no value to the coffee that may exist next year.

Trading summary: December in a 267–290c range, with 273.83c support and 283–286c resistance as the dividing lines. Key monitors: (1) daily certified-stock changes and the Brazilian rejection rate (about 15%), (2) whether hail assessments expand, (3) the shipment conversion rate on Cecafé's 2.07 million bags of certificate requests, (4) uniformity of the October second bloom, (5) robusta reclaiming $3,400, (6) USD/BRL toward 5.20, (7) the scale of new short accumulation below 280c.

For roasters and importers, three practical conclusions. First, reconsider paying a nearby premium to secure immediate delivery — with stocks rebuilding and the curve flattening, the value of urgent December procurement has fallen sharply, and spreading into March and May has become relatively more attractive. Second, manage flat price and differentials as entirely separate books — New York fell 2.5% while southern Minas physicals fell 1.1%, and a 21% sorting rate and 15% exchange rejection rate explain why. Third, establish 2027 upside exposure now — this was a week in which real risk rose while price fell. Flowering hail, a biennial down-year, multiple blooms and heat-wave warnings all point at 2027, and the market priced that risk more cheaply this week than at any point this quarter. The right frame is not a directional bet but an asymmetric option.
