Coffee Futures Market Weekly Update

This was a week in which Monday's spike was handed back over the following four sessions. December arabica (KCZ26) jumped +5.9% to 341.65c on Monday, its highest close since late January, then fell three straight sessions to a 3-week low of 309.65c on Thursday before rebounding +1.03% on Friday to settle at 312.85c. That leaves the week at -9.80c (-3.04%). The decisive turn came from full Brazilian warehouses — reports that most storage facilities had stopped accepting new coffee exposed the physical limit of the “farmers are holding back” narrative. In the same week, ICE certified arabica stocks fell to a 27-year low of 223,976 bags — and prices fell anyway.

Week in Review

The price path this week was a textbook inverted V. December arabica surged +19.00c (+5.9%) on Monday to 341.65c, its highest close since late January. London robusta November also jumped +5.1% to $3,802. Two catalysts drove it: slow commercialisation of Brazil's new crop and the relentless slide in ICE certified stocks (226,242 bags on Monday). On Tuesday the market printed a 7.5-month intraday high.

And then, from that very Tuesday, the market turned. Tuesday -1.80% (335.50c), Wednesday -3.98% (322.15c), Thursday -3.88% (309.65c) — 32.00c erased in three sessions. Friday's +1.03% rebound to 312.85c recovered part of the damage, but the week still closed -3.04%.

The trigger for the reversal was the report that most Brazilian warehouses have stopped accepting new coffee for lack of space. That single fact says two things at once. First, it is physical confirmation that this year's crop really is enormous. Second, and more importantly, it means producers who have been sitting on coffee waiting for better prices will be forced to sell as storage runs out. The “producer holding” logic that has propped the market up for two months has hit a physical ceiling.

The week's core dynamic: the single most important fact this week is that inventories and price moved in opposite directions. ICE certified stocks set a fresh 27-year low (lowest since 1999) at 223,976 bags, and arabica still fell -3.04% on the week. For months the market has traded the equation “falling certified stocks = higher prices”. This week that equation was neutralised by an opposing physical fact: full warehouses. The marginal information value of the inventory signal has started to decay — that is the week's most important structural change.

News Briefing

  • (Mon 8/24, market) December arabica surged +5.9% to 341.65c, the highest close since late January. Robusta November rose +5.1% to $3,802, a 2.5-week high. The drivers: slow new-crop selling in Brazil and certified stocks at a new low of 226,242 bags (Comunicaffe).
  • (Mon 8/24, FNC Colombia) The National Federation of Colombian Coffee Growers said El Niño, not the earthquake, is the biggest threat to the sector. “Production itself is not affected by the earthquake. Production will very likely be affected by the El Niño phenomenon,” said federation head Germán Bahamón. The FNC reported production down 23% y/y at 1.06 million 60kg bags, blaming adverse weather that delayed the harvest.
  • (Aug 25–27, Brazil / Reuters & Comunicaffe) Most Brazilian warehouses are no longer accepting new coffee as space fills up. At the same time growers continue to reject buyers' bids and postpone sales. This “overflowing but unsold” deadlock was the direct backdrop to the -7.9% two-day slide on Wednesday and Thursday.
  • (Wed 8/26, Sucafina / CEPEA) Sucafina's market report noted certified arabica stocks at 226,242 bags, close to the November 2023 low of about 224,000, and said a further drop would take them to their lowest since 1999 — which duly happened on Friday at 223,976. Separately, CEPEA confirmed the 2026/27 Brazilian crop is one of the poorest in recent years on quality, with a high incidence of lower-grade coffee.
  • (Wed 8/26, Reuters poll) A Reuters survey pointed to arabica around 300c/lb and robusta $3,900/t by year-end. Respondents expect the surplus to widen from 1.7 million bags in 2025/26 to 8.2 million in 2026/27, driven by Brazilian output up 17.2% to 75 million bags. They nonetheless cautioned that low carry-over stocks and growing demand from new markets such as China make a sharp collapse unlikely even with a large surplus.
  • (Wed 8/26, Cooxúpé / Expocacer) Cooxúpé reported the harvest 87.5% complete as of Aug 21, up 6 points on the week but still behind 91.3% a year earlier. In the Cerrado Mineiro, the Expocacer region's arabica harvest reached 87% versus roughly 90% last year.
  • (Fri 8/28, ICE) Certified arabica stocks 223,976 bags — a 27-year low, the lowest since 1999. By contrast, robusta stocks hit a 9-month high of 4,943 lots on Tuesday. The arabica–robusta inventory divergence widened for another week.
  • (Fri 8/28, J.M. Smucker) The owner of Folgers and Café Bustelo raised guidance, forecasting a smaller-than-expected annual sales decline. It said lower coffee prices allowed it to cut retail prices and that price-conscious consumers are increasing at-home consumption — the first earnings-based evidence that the end of the high-price regime is feeding back into demand recovery.

1. Inventories — a 27-year low, and prices fell anyway

Certified stocks kept draining: 227,992 bags on Aug 21 → 226,242 on Aug 24 → 224,617 on Aug 26 → 224,011 on Aug 27 → 223,976 on Aug 28, or -68.7% year-on-year. As Sucafina flagged, the series has now broken below the November 2023 low of about 224,000 bags and entered territory not seen since 1999.

But the more telling detail is the deceleration. Last week's drawdown was -8,438 bags (-3.6%); this week's was -4,016 bags (-1.76%) — less than half. Thursday's draw was just 606 bags and Friday's only 35, meaning the decline effectively stalled in the back half of the week. The inventory-drain momentum itself is fading.

Implication: what the market taught this week is that “record-low stocks = higher prices” no longer holds automatically. Prices fell 3% in the very week a 27-year low was set. The reason is straightforward — certified stocks measure coffee registered at the exchange, not coffee that exists in the world. With Brazilian warehouses overflowing, a record-low certified figure points to logistics, grading and quality bottlenecks, not to physical scarcity. Procurement teams should now treat Brazilian domestic stocks and commercialisation rates as the primary indicator, not the certified-stock headline.

2. Brazil — full warehouses start a forced-selling clock

The one fact that flipped the market this week is that most Brazilian warehouses have stopped taking in new coffee. It carries three layers of meaning.

First, it is physical confirmation of the record crop. The USDA puts Brazil's 2026/27 output at 71.9 million bags (+14%), Reuters poll respondents at 75 million (+17.2%), and CONAB at 66.7 million. The estimates disagree, but the fact that storage infrastructure has been overrun is an observation, not a forecast.

Second, the expiry clock on producer selling resistance has started running. Per Comunicaffe, Brazilian growers are still rejecting bids while they wait for a clearer market direction. But once warehouses are full, holding becomes physically impossible. Since that holding behaviour was the sole source of spot tightness over the past two months, its release is the release of the spot premium.

Third, the quality problem does not go away. CEPEA confirmed that the 2026/27 crop — hit by rain and humidity during harvesting — is one of the poorest in recent years on quality, with a high share of lower grades. In other words, volume will pour out, but the volume that passes exchange grading is a separate question.

The harvest is in its final phase. Cooxúpé progress moved from 81.1% on Aug 14 to 87.5% on Aug 21 (+6 points), still short of 91.3% a year ago. Expocacer's Cerrado region arabica stands at 87% versus about 90% last year. The national Safras & Mercado figure has not been updated since 90% as of Aug 12 (arabica 86%).

Implication: this is the week the “record crop plus record-low certified stocks” paradox began to resolve. The two facts have coexisted for months; full warehouses are the mechanism that forcibly ends that coexistence. But CEPEA's quality warning means the resolution will be incomplete. The realistic scenario is a widening quality spread: low grades released in volume, pressuring commercial and robusta-substitutable qualities, while premium washed and higher grades hold their premium. This is a market where procurement strategy has to be split by specification.

3. Weather — the focus shifts from harvest to premature flowering

Market attention has clearly moved from harvest progress to flowering-season weather. Comunicaffe reported concern that “isolated showers followed by intense heat and a new dry spell may lead to premature flowering and increase the risk of flower abortion”. That is the concrete transmission path for the “no rain in southern Minas” risk flagged in last week's report.

The backdrop is an El Niño expected to be among the strongest in more than 75 years. The US Climate Prediction Center said on July 8 that the current event is likely to rank that highly, and traders see delayed September–October rainfall during flowering as the key risk. Colombia's FNC explicitly ranked El Niño above the earthquake as a threat.

One caveat matters: no new Somar rainfall figure was confirmed this week. Even Barchart's Friday commentary still cites the 0.6mm (11% of the historical average) reading for the week ended Aug 16. Brazilian rainfall is therefore an unrefreshed data point relative to last week — and that information vacuum is itself the biggest source of volatility into next week.

4. Robusta — stocks at a 9-month high, underperformance persists

London robusta November peaked at $3,802 (+5.1%) on Monday, a 2.5-week high, then fell four sessions in a row to close at $3,529 on Friday. The -$89 (-2.46%) weekly loss was shallower than arabica's -3.04%, but Thursday brought a 2-month low.

The structural pressure is unambiguous. ICE certified robusta stocks hit a 9-month high of 4,943 lots on Tuesday. Vietnamese supply keeps building: January–July 2026 exports rose 21.1% to 1.31 MMT, and 2025/26 production is projected up 6% to a four-year high of 1.76 MMT (29.4 million bags). That the Reuters poll set a year-end robusta target of $3,900 — 10.5% above the current price, relatively higher than its arabica call — suggests the market reads robusta as having already absorbed much of its correction.

Inventory divergence update: arabica certified stocks at a 27-year low versus robusta at a 9-month high. Both extremes moved one notch further apart than last week (2.75-year low versus 8.75-month high). But because arabica prices fell this week, the divergence is transmitting into the price spread with less force. The arabica–robusta price gap did not narrow this week; it widened marginally (arabica -3.04% versus robusta -2.46%).

5. Demand — the first sign that lower prices are reviving consumption

The most underrated news of the week was J.M. Smucker's guidance upgrade. The owner of Folgers, Café Bustelo and at-home Dunkin' products said its annual sales decline would be smaller than expected, explaining that lower coffee prices allowed it to cut retail prices and that price-conscious consumers are increasing at-home consumption.

This is the first earnings-grounded evidence that demand destroyed during the 2024–2025 price spike is entering a recovery phase. It is also the basis for the Reuters poll's view that a sharp collapse is unlikely even with a large surplus — the logic being that low carry-over stocks plus demand growth in new markets such as China absorb the excess.

6. Regulation — EUDR in January 2027 puts a clock on rebuilding stocks

The editorial point raised by Perfect Daily Grind has real operational weight. Once the EU Deforestation Regulation takes effect in January 2027, rebuilding certified inventories carries additional administrative work. Exporters and roasters therefore have only a few months of a window in which to buy and ship this coffee without that burden.

The resulting setup is subtle. Full warehouses create pressure to release volume (bearish), while the EUDR deadline creates an incentive to certify and ship that volume quickly (accelerating stock recovery). A scenario in which certified stocks recover rapidly in Q4, removing the last bullish prop, cannot be ruled out.

7. FX — the real slightly softer, effectively neutral

USD/BRL sat at about 5.19 (5.186–5.194 across sources), marginally weaker than roughly 5.18 the prior week. A softer real raises producers' local-currency receipts and therefore strengthens the incentive to sell, which is directionally bearish — but at a move of roughly 0.2%, FX was effectively neutral for this week's price formation. With a far more powerful selling incentive already in play in the form of full warehouses, the marginal impact of the exchange rate is limited.

Implications

1. The “record-low certified stocks” signal is running out of life: prices fell 3.04% in the week a 27-year low was set. Certified stocks proved to be a function of registration, quality and logistics rather than physical balance. Replace the certified-stock headline with Brazilian commercialisation rates and warehouse availability as the primary input to procurement and hedging decisions. Certified stocks now belong in the lagging-confirmation category.

2. Full warehouses are a countdown to selling pressure: Brazilian producers' refusal to sell has been the only source of spot tightness for two months. When storage fills, holding becomes physically impossible. This is a bearish event with a definable timeline — build the procurement calendar around a base case of stepped Brazilian selling over the next four to eight weeks. For buyers, this is the start of a scale-in window.

3. The quality spread becomes the real game: CEPEA rates the 2026/27 Brazilian crop as among the worst in recent years on quality, with a high share of low grades. Volume release will therefore be anything but uniform across grades. Expect sharp pressure on commercial and lower grades, with premium washed and top grades holding their premium. Futures hedges cannot cover that basis, so locking physical contracts early, by specification, has gained relative value.

4. September–October flowering is the only large upside left: the bearish inputs are physical and structural, while the bullish case reduces to one variable — flowering weather. The market has already begun pricing a specific pathway (premature flowering → flower abortion) against a backdrop of the strongest El Niño in 75 years. Because no fresh Somar data was confirmed this week, the information vacuum makes next week's rainfall report an asymmetrically large event. Holding upside exposure via calls from the lower end of the range is the risk-adjusted way to stay positioned.

5. Demand recovery limits the downside: Smucker's guidance upgrade is proof of the retail price cut → consumption recovery channel. It is also why the Reuters poll sees no collapse even with an 8.2-million-bag surplus. The year-end consensus of about 300c sits just 4% below Friday's 312.85c settle — meaning even the consensus does not see much further downside from here. The risk-reward on aggressive short positions has deteriorated accordingly.

Outlook & Risks

Near-term structure (1–3 months)

On the December contract the trend has shifted to a wide range with a downward bias. Support runs 309.65c (Thursday's low close) · 300c (Reuters poll year-end target and psychological level) · 290c; resistance runs 322.15c (Wednesday's close) · 335.50c (Tuesday's close) · 341.65c (week's high). Friday's rebound defended Thursday's low, which matters near term, but a break of 309.65c leaves a wide gap down to 300c.

Technical view: the weekly closing range was 309.65–341.65c, a 32.00c (-9.4%) swing — volatility has expanded. A -9.4% move over three sessions from Monday's high to Thursday's low is a clear trend-reversal pattern. Friday's +1.03% looks like a technical retracement rather than a resumption of the uptrend. Failure to reclaim the 312–322c zone opens a test of 300c; conversely, a recovery of 322c puts 335c back in play.

Medium-term outlook (3–12 months)

The medium-term picture tilted clearly bearish this week. Two new inputs were added: (1) physical confirmation of the record crop via full warehouses, and (2) an expiry clock on producer selling resistance. These are reinforced by (3) the Reuters poll's 8.2-million-bag surplus for 2026/27 (up from 1.7 million in 2025/26). Layer on (4) a Q4 certification and shipping rush ahead of the EUDR deadline, and a rapid rebuild of certified stocks becomes plausible.

Three buffers remain: (1) the quality deterioration CEPEA identified, which may limit how much volume passes grading; (2) El Niño risk in the September–October flowering window; and (3) demand recovering in response to lower prices (the Smucker evidence). The base case stays “gradual drift lower punctuated by flowering-event spikes”, but this week's warehouse confirmation steepened the slope of that drift. Notably, the consensus year-end level of about 300c is only 4% below spot — the market agrees on the direction but is sceptical about the magnitude.

Key risk factors

  • Brazilian warehouse saturation (bearish, top priority): most facilities have stopped intake. The physical expiry of producer holding is imminent, and stepped selling is the core bearish scenario for the next four to eight weeks.
  • September–October flowering weather (bullish, the only large catalyst): premature flowering → flower abortion is already a stated market concern, against the strongest El Niño in 75 years. The absence of fresh Somar data makes next week's report unusually heavily weighted.
  • ICE certified stocks (weakening signal): 223,976 bags is a 27-year low, but the weekly draw halved from -3.6% to -1.76%. This week confirmed a regime in which inventories no longer function as price support.
  • Brazilian crop quality (bullish / structural): among the worst in recent years per CEPEA, with a high share of low grades. A record crop is not the same as record deliverable volume. A driver of wider quality spreads.
  • EUDR effective January 2027 (two-way): a pre-deadline certification and shipping rush accelerates Q4 stock recovery (bearish); the post-deadline administrative burden slows any rebuild (bullish).
  • Robusta (bearish): stocks at a 9-month high (4,943 lots), Vietnamese January–July exports +21.1%, new crop arriving in October. That said, the Reuters target of $3,900 is 10.5% above spot, implying the correction is largely absorbed.
  • Demand recovery (downside buffer): Smucker's guidance upgrade shows retail price cuts feeding consumption. Low carry-over stocks plus new-market demand partially absorb the surplus.
  • Colombia (bullish / limited): the FNC ranks El Niño above the earthquake as a threat; production is down 23% y/y at 1.06 million bags. Buenaventura remains partially reopened.

What to watch next week

  • Somar weekly rainfall report (Monday) — the first new data in two weeks. Whether Minas Gerais rainfall recovers toward the historical average, and a region-by-region read on southern Minas in particular, is the top priority.
  • Brazilian warehouse availability and commercialisation rates — whether intake suspensions spread, and whether producer selling actually rises, is the week's central question.
  • The 220,000-bag line on ICE certified stocks and pending-grading volumes — whether the slowdown in drawdowns turns into net builds.
  • CFTC COT (Aug 25 and Sep 1 dates) — the Aug 25 figure we could not confirm this week, plus the scale of position liquidation through the slide. Whether fresh shorts entered is the key question.
  • 309.65c support and any attempt to reclaim 322c — a break of Thursday's low leaves a technical vacuum down to 300c.
  • Preliminary August Cecafé exports and the delayed ICO monthly statistics — the first read on how fast the record crop converts into shipments.
  • Cooxúpé harvest progress (87.5% toward 90%+) — how fast the gap to last year's 91.3% closes.

Conclusion

This was the week the market discarded a narrative it had leaned on for months. Monday's +5.9% spike was the last expression of the familiar bullish logic of record-low certified stocks plus reluctant producers. But as 32.00c (-9.4%) evaporated between Tuesday and Thursday, the market rediscovered a much simpler truth: however low inventories go, prices do not rise when Brazilian warehouses are overflowing. That the week closed -3.04% in the very week ICE certified stocks set a 27-year low of 223,976 bags is the compressed conclusion of this report.

Structurally, the axis of the supply-demand debate has moved from availability to absorption capacity. For the past year the question was “is there enough coffee?” The question now is “when, at what grade and to where does the excess get pushed?” Full warehouses are the physical starting gun for that shift. At the same time, the quality deterioration CEPEA identified signals that the release will not be uniform — low grades will pour out while top grades stay scarce.

Trading summary: December range 300–335c, with 309.65c (Thursday's low close) as the first line. Key monitors: (1) Brazilian warehouse availability and the turn in producer selling; (2) September–October flowering rainfall (Monday's Somar report); (3) whether the slowing inventory draw turns into net builds; (4) the widening of physical basis by quality grade; (5) the Q4 certification rush ahead of EUDR; (6) whether robusta stocks keep setting highs.

For roasters and importers there are three operational takeaways. First, demote the certified-stock headline from the primary procurement indicator and replace it with Brazilian commercialisation rates and warehouse availability. Second, split procurement strategy by specification — wait out the decline in commercial grades, but lock premium washed lots early on the assumption of widening premiums. Third, weight the bearish scenario while retaining flowering-season upside exposure. The bearish drivers (full warehouses, a widening surplus, price cuts that revive demand) are structural and forecastable; the bullish driver (El Niño damage during flowering) is low probability but large in magnitude. That the consensus year-end level sits only 4% below spot supports the conclusion that a downward-biased range strategy dominates an outright short.
